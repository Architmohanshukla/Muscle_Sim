The workbook sweeps the whole position range and writes one row per position, limb and muscle — activation, both length measures, tendon strain and force, side by side so they pivot directly. Separate sheets carry joint angles and moments, tendon and joint loads, and the muscle architecture table. The CSV is the muscle sheet on its own.

How to read this

Activation is how hard the nervous system is driving the muscle, chosen by minimising total squared activation subject to every joint moment being met exactly.

Fibre length is where the contractile fibres sit relative to their optimal length, solved from fibre–tendon force equilibrium. MTU is the whole muscle–tendon unit's length change, which is pure geometry. These two disagree on purpose: the quadriceps unit lengthens about 30 % into a full squat while its fibres lengthen far less, because the tendon and aponeurosis take the rest.

Tension splits into active and passive. Passive tension needs no activation at all — it is the price of being stretched.

None of these is joint compression. A joint is compressed by the muscles crossing it plus the bone-on-bone force, which is why the patellofemoral joint is squeezed hardest exactly where the quadriceps has the least leverage.

Every movement runs on the same engine. The squat and split squat are lower-body chains; the curl and push-up add a fully articulated upper limb. The split squat is the only one solved as two independent limbs, because its two legs genuinely do different jobs.

Depth is named by the hip, not the knee. With the shank inclined ~35°, a horizontal thigh needs about 125° of knee flexion — so “90 degrees” is a half squat with the hip still 22 cm above the knee, not parallel. Drag to 100 % for a true full squat: hip 22 cm below the knee.

Knee loading peaks at parallel, not at the bottom. Patellofemoral compression rises to 3.6 × body weight around parallel and then falls to about 2 × BW at full depth, because the back of the thigh wedges against the calf and takes over part of the knee extension demand. Watch the numbers as you pass 130°.

Model detail

How each stage is computed

Posture. Each movement solves its own. In the squat, knee flexion is the driver and ankle dorsiflexion is solved so the COM sits over the centre of pressure, making trunk lean and required ankle range predictions rather than inputs. In the split squat, both feet are planted, so stance length caps how high the hips can be and the front knee angle is solved from how far the rear leg has folded. In the curl, the arm hangs under gravity and trunk lean is solved from balance — which is why the model leans back under a heavy dumbbell. In the push-up, elbow flexion sets the shoulder-to-wrist distance while the shoulder must stay a fixed distance from the toe contact: two circles, one intersection. Segment masses and inertias are de Leva (1996).

Open vs closed chains. The squat and curl are grounded at one end, so virtual power handles them directly. The split squat and push-up have two ground contacts. The push-up is still determinate — the plank is rigid, so moments about the toes fix the hand reaction. The split squat is genuinely indeterminate: how much weight goes through each foot is the lifter’s choice, so it is a prescribed, measured quantity exposed as a control. Its per-leg joint moments then come from standard bottom-up inverse dynamics, which is verified against the squat’s virtual-power result to 0.002 N·m.

Moments. The planar chain is parameterised by exactly the joint angles we want moments about, so d'Alembert's principle gives each joint moment directly as Σ m(a − g)·∂r/∂q, with Jacobians taken by central differences on the forward kinematics. In With inertia mode the segment accelerations come from the actual trajectory.

Muscle geometry. All 55 muscles — 28 lower limb, 27 upper — carry a measured moment arm r(θ) about every degree of freedom they cross, and length follows by virtual work, L = Lref − ∫r dθ. Length and leverage therefore cannot contradict each other.

Muscle model. Hill-type with an elastic tendon: Gaussian active force–length, exponential passive force–length, Hill force–velocity, variable pennation, and a fibre–tendon equilibrium solved per muscle per frame.

Recruitment. Because force is linear in activation at a fixed fibre state, each frame is a bound-constrained quadratic program, solved exactly by an active-set method, wrapped in a fixed-point loop over tendon equilibrium. Constraint residuals are printed above and are normally below 0.01 N·m.

Limitations — read before trusting a number
  • Not OpenSim. This is planar, bilaterally symmetric, and uses moment-arm splines instead of three-dimensional muscle paths and wrapping surfaces. Treat it as a teaching instrument, not a subject-specific analysis.
  • Synergist splits are unreliable; group totals are better. Minimising squared activation is winner-take-most between muscles that do the same job. The model gives hip extension almost entirely to gluteus maximus and near-zero to adductor magnus, whereas EMG shows both working. Read hip extensors as a group, not the individual split.
  • Out-of-plane demands are estimates. Hip ab/adduction, hip rotation and ankle inversion moments are first-order estimates from stance geometry, not measured data; muscles acting mainly about them are tagged in the table. The deep hip external rotators are outside the requested muscle set, so hip rotation demand is deliberately kept conservative.
  • Stabiliser activation is partly imposed. Pure effort minimisation silences every stabiliser, so frontal/transverse muscles and the abdominal wall carry a co-activation floor scaled to joint demand. Rows sitting on it are tagged floor and are not predictions.
  • Passive properties are calibrated, not measured. Passive fibre strain was set per muscle group so the model's passive joint moments match measured end-range values (knee 10–20, hip 15–35, ankle 10–20 N·m). Passive ligament and disc contributions to lumbar resistance are not modelled, so lumbar passive moment reads low.
  • Thigh-calf contact is a modelled estimate. Past ~130° of knee flexion the posterior soft tissues wedge together and offload the knee extensors. It is represented as a quadratic contact spring calibrated to a contact force of ~0.4 × body weight at full depth, which is inside the reported range, but it is not measured for this subject — and it depends strongly on how bulky the thigh and calf are. It is reported as its own row so you can see its size.
  • Bottom-position activations are a lower bound. Effort minimisation is happy to rest the body on passive tissue and thigh-calf contact, so the model shows very low quadriceps and glute activity at the very bottom. That is real for a relaxed resting squat, but a controlled rep carries more activation than this at the bottom. The ascent figures are the more representative ones.
  • Joint contact forces are estimates. Patellofemoral force uses the standard published F = Fquad · k(θ) relation, which is well supported. Tibiofemoral, hip and disc loads resolve muscle forces onto bone axes with documented approximations rather than true anatomy.
  • The shoulder is not a planar joint. Elbow and shoulder flexion are well posed here. The rotator cuff and scapular muscles act about rotation and scapular degrees of freedom whose demands in these movements are first-order estimates, and the scapula has no independent kinematics — it is driven from the humerus by a fixed scapulohumeral rhythm. Read those rows as "this group is working", not as a reliable split between individual muscles.
  • Forearm rotation is not constrained in the curl. A dumbbell held at its centre applies no twist, and whatever net twist the muscles produce is taken by the radioulnar end stop and the grip. The grip’s real effect — the biceps losing about a third of its elbow leverage when pronated, and changing length — is modelled.
  • One subject. 75 kg, 1.75 m. Anthropometry and joint range limits are constants in the configuration block, not controls.
